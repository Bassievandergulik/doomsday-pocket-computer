# Radio guide: what to listen to

When internet and mobile networks fail, **broadcast radio is how governments give instructions**. Battery FM/AM radios are part of every official EU emergency kit.

## Radio bands
This device's Si4732 receiver covers **FM, MW, LW and shortwave**, and with the SSB patch also **USB/LSB** (radio amateurs, weather fax and text). Use the long wire antenna for MW/LW/SW; the short wire is enough for FM.

| Band | Range | What you hear | Range of reception |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FM** | 87.5–108 MHz | National and regional stations, best sound, RDS station names | 30–100 km, line of sight |
| **MW (AM)** | 522–1710 kHz | Few stations left in Western Europe; strong foreign stations at night | 100 km by day, 1000+ km at night |
| **LW** | 153–279 kHz | Very few remaining stations (e.g. 198 kHz BBC R4 UK, being phased out) | Hundreds of km |
| **SW** | 2.3–26 MHz | International broadcasters, news from other continents | Thousands of km |

## Emergency broadcasting
- **Netherlands**: the designated **calamity broadcaster (calamiteitenzender)** is your **regional public station** (e.g. Omroep Gelderland, NH Radio, Radio Rijnmond, Omroep Brabant, L1, RTV Noord) and NPO Radio 1 nationally. In an emergency they interrupt programmes with official instructions. Find your regional station's FM frequency now and save it as a preset.
- **Belgium**: VRT Radio 1/Radio 2 (Flemish), RTBF La Première (French).
- **Germany**: the regional ARD station (WDR, NDR, BR, SWR, MDR, HR...) carries official warnings.
- **UK**: BBC Radio 4 and BBC local radio.
- In NL the public warning sirens are tested on the first Monday of the month at 12:00 (if your area still has them; NL-Alert phone messages are the main channel). A siren at any other time means: **go inside, close doors and windows, switch off ventilation, tune in to the regional station.** ("Ga naar binnen, sluit ramen en deuren, zet de radio aan.")

## Shortwave tips
- Best by day: **15–21 MHz (19 m, 16 m, 13 m bands)**. Best at night: **5.9–7.6 MHz (49 m, 41 m)**. 9–12 MHz (31 m, 25 m) works around dusk and dawn.
- Use a long wire antenna (5–10 m of wire clipped to the antenna input, run as high as possible, ideally outside).
- Station schedules change every year (March and October); use "Scan band" to find what's on now and save presets.
- Broadcasters on SW to Europe in recent years have included Radio Romania International, China Radio International, Voice of Turkey, Radio Exterior de España, Radio Habana Cuba and various religious stations. Many news services have left shortwave.

## Weather on the radio
**Weather charts and text by shortwave:** this device can receive the weather fax charts and text bulletins that weather services send to ships, with no internet. See [Weather fax and text by radio](page:comms/weather-fax).

Europe has no NOAA-style weather channel on FM. Weather comes in **news bulletins** (usually on the hour). National weather services (KNMI, KMI/IRM, DWD, Met Office) issue warnings via the public broadcasters: code yellow/orange/red. Combine with this device's own barometer forecast (Weather tab).

## Getting better reception
- FM: extend the wire antenna to about 75 cm (quarter wave), hold it vertical, go high (upstairs, hilltop), near a window.
- MW/LW (other radios): the ferrite rod antenna is directional: rotate the radio for the strongest signal.
- Keep away from switching power supplies, LED drivers, solar inverters: they cause noise.

## Two-way radio (not built in)
| Type | Licence | Use |
|---|---|---|
| PMR446 | none (EU) | 16 channels, 446.0–446.2 MHz, 0.5 W, 1–5 km. Agree a channel and a check-in time with family (e.g. channel 3, every hour at :00 for 5 minutes). |
| CB 27 MHz | none in most EU countries | 40 channels, ch 9 emergency, ch 19 road. 5–20 km. |
| Ham radio | exam required | Long range, organised emergency networks. In real distress anyone may use any means to call for help. |
